How much water does it take to make a power bank?

20–55 litresto manufacture one power bank

That's one to four weeks of one person's drinking water, used before the power bank has charged anything. The range depends on its size and what the casing is made of.

Two ways to count it

You can count the water that goes into the device, or the water behind every phone charge it gives you over its life. They tell different stories.

The smaller, cheaper one is the thirstier one. The bigger power bank takes more water to make, but it lasts far longer, so each charge costs three to four times less water.

One catch: power banks are typically replaced within two years, often long before they wear out. The lowest-water power bank is the one you keep.

Where the water goes

Most of it isn't used in the battery factory. It's upstream, in the metals.

  • Cathode materials, 55%. Mostly cobalt and nickel. Cobalt mined in Congo is processed using hydropower, and water evaporating from the reservoirs is counted.
  • Aluminium, 22%. The foil and cell cans. Smelting aluminium takes huge amounts of electricity, often from hydropower. An aluminium casing pushes this share much higher.
  • Cell production, 13%. Making and conditioning the battery cells.
  • Everything else, 10%. Graphite, copper, electrolyte, plastics.

Shares are for the battery cells, calculated from Argonne National Laboratory's battery life cycle model for a lithium-ion battery with NMC cathode chemistry.

Why cheap tends to cost more water

The studies don't compare power banks by price. They compare chemistry, cell type and lifespan, and budget models tend to lose on all three.

  • Chemistry. Low-cost electronics commonly use lithium cobalt oxide cells, which contain much more cobalt than the chemistry our figures are based on. Our budget numbers are likely an underestimate.
  • Cell type. Power banks built from flat pouch cells had around 66% lower production impacts per unit of energy delivered than ones built from cylindrical cells, thanks to higher energy density and longer life.
  • Lifespan. Rated cycle life among 50 best-selling models ranged from about 300 to over 800 full charges.

What about lithium?

You may have seen claims that lithium takes anywhere from a few hundred to two million litres of water per kilo. The disagreement is mostly about what counts as water.

Much of the world's lithium comes from salty underground brine, pumped into ponds and left to evaporate. In the Argonne model, the lithium in the cathode adds under 3 litres per kilo of cathode material. Our reading is that evaporated brine isn't counted as freshwater there, while the headline-grabbing figures do count it. Same lithium, wildly different answers, depending on where you draw the line.

That's the problem with industrial water in general: it's rarely measured the same way twice.

How we worked it out

We counted freshwater consumed during manufacturing, from mining the raw materials to the finished power bank. That's water taken out and not returned, including evaporation. We did not count water used to charge it or to dispose of it.

What's not included

  • Pollution. Water needed to dilute contaminated discharges from mining and processing (often called grey water) isn't in these numbers, so the true figure is higher.
  • Brine. Evaporated lithium brine, as explained above.
  • Per-charge figures assume full charge cycles and the manufacturer's rated cycle life, which real use rarely reaches.
